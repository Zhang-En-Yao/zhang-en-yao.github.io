## Madrid

Lodging: Ronda de Atocha 28

Madrid enters the written record as *Mayrit*, a fortress and small walled town founded by the Umayyad emir Muhammad I of Córdoba around 860–880 to guard the approaches to Toledo. Alfonso VI of Castile took it around 1085 in the same campaign that won Toledo. For four centuries it remained a secondary Castilian town until Philip II moved his permanent court there in 1561, a decision that turned a place with no cathedral, no university and no port into the capital of a global monarchy almost overnight. The city's historic core is therefore a compressed one: a Habsburg "Madrid de los Austrias" of brick, slate spires and arcaded plazas laid out between roughly 1560 and 1700, overlaid by the Bourbon "Madrid de los Borbones" of stone palaces, boulevards, museums and academies built after 1700 in a deliberate campaign to give the capital a monumental face.

### Royal Palace & the Old Quarter

The Habsburg-era old town centered on the Royal Palace — churches, plazas, and the market all within walking distance.

#### Palacio Real de Madrid
*Sight · Visit 10/24*

The site has been the seat of power in Madrid since the 9th century, when the Muslim citadel (*al-mudayna*) stood on this bluff above the Manzanares. After the Christian conquest the Castilian kings kept a fortified *alcázar* here; it was rebuilt as a Renaissance and early-Baroque royal residence under Charles V and Philip II. That building burned to the ground on Christmas Eve 1734, during the reign of the first Bourbon king, Philip V, taking with it a large part of the royal art collection.

Philip V used the disaster to commission a wholly new palace in the Italian and French manner. The Turin architect Filippo Juvarra drew the first, gigantic project; after his death in 1736 his pupil Giovanni Battista Sacchetti reduced it to the present square block built around a central courtyard, on a plinth of Guadarrama granite and Colmenar limestone chosen to be fireproof. Construction ran from 1738 to 1755; Charles III moved his court in in 1764, and later Bourbon architects — Ventura Rodríguez, Francesco Sabatini — completed the interiors and surroundings. With about 135,000 m² of floor area and some 3,400 rooms it is the largest functioning royal palace in Western Europe.

Today the palace is used by the Crown only for state ceremonies, official banquets and receptions; the royal family lives at the Palacio de la Zarzuela outside the city. Its historic apartments, the Royal Armoury, the Royal Pharmacy, and works by Caravaggio, Velázquez, Goya and Tiepolo (who frescoed the throne-room ceiling) are open to the public as a museum administered by Patrimonio Nacional.

#### Catedral de la Almudena
*Sight · Visit 10/24*

Madrid had no cathedral for the first three centuries of its life as capital, because it was never a bishopric — it remained under the Archdiocese of Toledo until 1885. Plans for a cathedral dedicated to the Virgin of the Almudena (the name derives from the Arabic *al-mudayna*, "the citadel," where an image of the Virgin was said to have been hidden during the Muslim period and rediscovered in 1085) were repeatedly proposed and shelved.

King Alfonso XII laid the first stone on 4 April 1883, to a Neo-Gothic design by Francisco de Cubas, Marqués de Cubas, originally conceived as a grand burial church for his late queen, María de las Mercedes. Work was slow and repeatedly interrupted; the crypt opened for worship in 1911, and construction halted almost entirely during the economic crises of the 1920s–30s and the Civil War. A 1944 competition was won by Fernando Chueca Goitia and Carlos Sidro, who redesigned the unbuilt upper church in a Neoclassical idiom to harmonise with the Royal Palace opposite, lowering the nave so as not to overtop it. The exterior was finished only in 1960 and the interior in the 1990s.

Pope John Paul II consecrated the completed cathedral on 15 June 1993 — the first cathedral consecrated by a pope outside Rome in modern times — more than a century after it was begun. The result is stylistically layered: Neo-Gothic crypt, Neoclassical Bailén-façade and dome, and a starkly modern painted interior with a coloured ceiling by Kiko Argüello.

#### Plaza de Oriente
*Plaza · Visit 10/24*

The sloping ground between the Royal Palace and the church of San Gil was cleared by Joseph Bonaparte, Napoleon's brother, who ruled Spain from 1808 to 1813 and demolished so many convents and blocks to open up public squares that Madrileños nicknamed him *el Rey Plazuelas* ("the King of Little Squares"). His scheme was left unfinished at his fall; the present elliptical, gardened plaza was completed under Isabel II to a design by Narciso Pascual y Colomer and opened in 1844.

At its centre stands the bronze equestrian statue of Philip IV, cast in Florence in 1634–40 by Pietro Tacca after a portrait sketch by Velázquez. It is the first large equestrian statue in the world balanced solely on the horse's hind legs; Tacca is said to have consulted Galileo Galilei on the distribution of weight, and the horse was accordingly cast solid at the rear and hollow at the front. The statue stood in the gardens of the vanished Buen Retiro palace before being moved here in 1843. Ringing the square are limestone statues of Visigothic and early Spanish monarchs, carved in the 1750s for the cornice of the Royal Palace but placed at ground level because, by tradition, Queen Bárbara de Braganza feared they would blow down in a storm.

#### Plaza Mayor
*Plaza · Visit 10/24*

Madrid's arcaded main square occupies the site of a medieval market square, the Plaza del Arrabal, just outside the original town wall. Philip II commissioned a regular, enclosed plaza from his royal architect Juan de Herrera; work began in 1580 but the square was completed only under Philip III, to a revised design by Juan Gómez de Mora, and inaugurated in 1619. The bronze equestrian statue of Philip III at the centre, by Giambologna and Tacca, was another Florentine gift, placed here in 1848.

The enclosed space — 129 by 94 metres, ringed by uniform four- and five-storey residential blocks with 237 wrought-iron balconies — was the ceremonial theatre of Habsburg Madrid. It hosted royal proclamations and coronations, the canonisation festivities of 1622 (Isidore, Ignatius of Loyola, Francis Xavier, Teresa of Ávila), bullfights, Carnival, markets and the *autos de fe* of the Spanish Inquisition. Three major fires — 1631, 1672 and 1790 — successively destroyed much of the fabric; after the last, Juan de Villanueva rebuilt it in its present form, lowering the ranges, closing the corners with arched entries and giving it the granite ground-floor arcade seen today. The Casa de la Panadería, the frescoed building on the north side, is the only survivor with its original 17th-century towers.

#### Mercado de San Miguel
*Sight · Visit 10/24*

The market stands on the site of the church of San Miguel de los Octoes, demolished in 1810 under Joseph Bonaparte; the cleared space served as an open-air food market through the 19th century. The present hall was built in 1913–16 by the architect Alfonso Dubé y Díez, who took the iron-and-glass market halls of Paris (Les Halles, 1857) as his model, aiming at a hygienic, well-lit, well-ventilated space for a fast-growing city. It was inaugurated on 13 May 1916.

It is the last surviving cast-iron market hall in central Madrid and is listed as a *Bien de Interés Cultural*. After decades of decline it closed in the 1990s; a private consortium restored the structure and reopened it in 2009 not as a neighbourhood produce market but as a *mercado gastronómico* — stalls selling prepared tapas, oysters, jamón ibérico, vermouth and wine for eating on the spot.

#### La Latina
*District · Visit 10/25*

This is the oldest inhabited part of Madrid: the tangle of narrow, curving lanes on the southern slope below the Royal Palace preserves the street pattern of the Muslim and medieval town, outside the first wall. The square names still record its trades — Plaza de la Cebada (barley), Plaza de la Paja (straw), Plaza de los Carros (carts). The district is named after Beatriz Galindo (c. 1465–1534), a humanist and Latin scholar so accomplished that she was known as *La Latina*; tutor to Queen Isabella I, she and her husband Francisco Ramírez founded a hospital here in 1499 whose name eventually attached to the whole quarter. The hospital was demolished in 1904.

Just to the south, El Rastro — held every Sunday morning along the Ribera de Curtidores — is Madrid's great flea market, running since at least the 18th century. Its name (*rastro*, "trail") recalls the blood trail left by animals dragged from the nearby slaughterhouses and tanneries that once defined this working-class edge of the city.

#### Real Basílica de San Francisco el Grande
*Church · Visit 10/25*

A Franciscan friary, traditionally said to have been founded by Francis of Assisi himself on a visit in 1217, occupied this site for centuries. Charles III had it rebuilt on a monumental scale as a royal church. The Franciscan friar-architect Francisco Cabezas designed a vast circular plan crowned by a dome and began work in 1761, but was dismissed after seven years in a dispute over whether so wide a dome could stand. Charles III's architect Francesco Sabatini and his assistant Miguel Fernández completed it, and the church was consecrated in 1784.

Its coffered dome is 33 metres in diameter — the largest in Spain and among the largest in Christendom, after St Peter's, the Pantheon and Florence. In the 19th century the state confiscated the friary and briefly considered making the church a national pantheon; it was redecorated with large history paintings and murals by leading 19th-century Spanish artists. Among its earlier holdings is a *San Bernardino of Siena* (1783) painted by the young Goya, who included a self-portrait. Pope John XXIII raised it to a minor basilica in 1963.

### Barrio de las Letras & the Art Triangle

The old quarter where Spain's Golden Age writers lived, running straight into the Prado art triangle — theaters, writers' houses, and museums all cluster here.

#### Puerta del Sol
*Plaza · Visit 10/23*

The "Gate of the Sun" was one of the gates in Madrid's 15th-century wall, named for a sun carved or painted on it and facing east. As the city outgrew its walls the gate disappeared and the space became a busy meeting of roads and a centre of gossip and public life. Its present importance dates from the Casa de Correos, the royal post house built in 1766–68 by the French architect Jaime Marquet, now the seat of the regional government of Madrid; its clock tower (1866) is the one the whole country watches on New Year's Eve, when Spaniards eat twelve grapes on the twelve strokes of midnight, a custom fixed here in 1909.

In front of the building a paved marker records *Kilómetro Cero*, the origin point from which Spain's six radial national highways (N-I to N-VI) are measured; the first plaque was set in 1950. The square has been the stage for pivotal events: the popular rising against French troops on 2 May 1808, the proclamation of the Second Republic in 1931, and the *15-M* protest encampment of 2011. A statue of the bear and the strawberry tree (*madroño*), the city's heraldic emblem, stands at the eastern end.

#### Barrio de Las Letras
*District · Visit 10/23*

This quarter east of Puerta del Sol was the residential and professional heart of Spain's literary Golden Age (roughly 1580–1680). Miguel de Cervantes, Lope de Vega, Francisco de Quevedo, Luis de Góngora and Pedro Calderón de la Barca all lived within these few blocks, drawn by the two open-air playhouses — the Corral de la Cruz and the Corral del Príncipe — and by the printing shops around Calle de Atocha, where Juan de la Cuesta printed the first edition of *Don Quixote* in 1604–05. The district's modern name and its literary-tourism identity were promoted from the early 20th century; bronze lines of Golden Age verse are inlaid in the pavement throughout.

#### Calle de Cervantes
*Street · Visit 10/23*

One of the two principal streets of the literary quarter. By a well-known irony of Madrid's street-naming, the house of Lope de Vega stands on Calle de Cervantes, while Cervantes' burial place lies on Calle de Lope de Vega. Cervantes spent his last years and died (1616) in a house on the corner of this street and Calle del León; the building is gone, marked only by a plaque.

#### Calle de Lope de Vega
*Street · Visit 10/23*

The street runs past the Convento de las Trinitarias Descalzas, a cloistered convent founded in 1612 whose church holds the unmarked grave of Cervantes. He had asked to be buried there in gratitude to the Trinitarian order, which had ransomed him from captivity in Algiers in 1580. His remains were disturbed by 17th-century rebuilding; a forensic search in 2015 identified bone fragments consistent with him, now reinterred behind a memorial. The nuns still live enclosed in the convent.

#### Casa Museo Lope de Vega
*Museum · Visit 10/23*

Félix Lope de Vega (1562–1635), the most prolific playwright of the Golden Age — hundreds of surviving plays, and a life as turbulent as any of them — bought this house on what is now Calle de Cervantes in 1610 and lived here until his death. Later owners altered it beyond recognition; the Real Academia Española acquired it in 1930, restored the 17th-century layout under the scholar Agustín González de Amezúa, and opened it as a house-museum in 1935, the year it was declared a national monument. The reconstructed rooms, the oratory, the study and the small back garden Lope called *mi güertecillo* are furnished in period style; the garden replanting follows plants named in his own writings.

#### Corral de Comedias de la Cruz
*Theater · Visit 10/23*

A commemorative plaque on Calle de la Cruz marks the site of the Corral de la Cruz, opened in 1579 as Madrid's first permanent public playhouse and, with the Corral del Príncipe (1583), the venue where the drama of Lope, Tirso de Molina and Calderón was first performed. The *corral* was an open courtyard between tenement houses: a raised stage at one end, standing room (the *patio*) for the common audience, a screened gallery (*cazuela*) for women, and the windows and balconies of the flanking houses rented as boxes. Both theatres were run for the profit of Madrid's charitable brotherhoods and hospitals. The Cruz playhouse operated, with rebuildings, until 1859; the Teatro Español on Plaza de Santa Ana stands on the site of its rival, the Príncipe.

#### Plaza de Santa Ana
*Plaza · Visit 10/23*

Until 1810 this block was occupied by the convent, church and gardens of the Discalced Carmelite nuns of San José y Santa Ana, founded in 1586 by disciples of Teresa of Ávila. Joseph Bonaparte demolished it in 1810 and laid out the square — one of his many *plazuelas* — to a design by Silvestre Pérez. On the west side stands the Teatro Español, direct heir of the Corral del Príncipe and the oldest continuously operating theatre in Madrid; opposite is the Hotel Reina Victoria, long associated with bullfighters. Statues of Calderón de la Barca (1878) and Federico García Lorca preside over the square, which today is a centre of the *tapeo* and evening drinking scene.

#### Teatro de la Zarzuela
*Theater · Visit 10/23*

Built in 1856 by a company of aristocratic and artistic backers to give the specifically Spanish form of *zarzuela* — a lyric-theatre genre alternating sung numbers with spoken dialogue, its roots in the 17th century — a home of its own, independent of the Italian opera at the Teatro Real. Jerónimo de la Gándara designed it on the horseshoe model of La Scala; it was raised in barely seven months and opened on 10 October 1856, the birthday of Queen Isabel II. Fire gutted the interior in 1909, after which it was rebuilt. It remains Spain's principal zarzuela house and is a state-run national theatre.

#### Museo del Prado
*Museum · Visit 10/25*

The building was designed in 1785 by the Neoclassical architect Juan de Villanueva, on the tree-lined *paseo del Prado*, to house Charles III's Natural History Cabinet and an academy of sciences. Construction was wrecked during the Peninsular War, when French troops used the shell as a barracks and stripped its lead roof. Ferdinand VII, prompted by his wife María Isabel de Braganza, decided instead to use it to display the royal painting collection; it opened as the Royal Museum of Paintings on 19 November 1819 with 311 works on show.

The collection's character follows the taste of the Habsburg and Bourbon kings: the deepest holdings anywhere of Velázquez (*Las Meninas*), Goya (the *Black Paintings*, the two *Majas*, *The Third of May 1808*), El Greco, Ribera, Zurbarán and Murillo; Titian and the Venetians collected by Charles V and Philip II; Bosch's *Garden of Earthly Delights*; major Rubens, Dürer and Rembrandt. Nationalised in 1868, enriched by dissolved convents and later bequests, and expanded in 2007 by a Rafael Moneo wing behind the church of San Jerónimo el Real, it is regarded as one of the two or three greatest painting museums in the world.

### Elsewhere

#### Ermita de San Antonio de la Florida
*Church · Visit 10/25*

A small Neoclassical chapel by the Manzanares, built in 1792–98 for Charles IV. In the summer of 1798 Francisco de Goya, then First Court Painter, frescoed its dome, pendentives and vaults in a single campaign of a few months. Instead of the expected celestial hierarchy, Goya painted the miracle of St Anthony of Padua — the saint raising a murdered man to testify to his father's innocence — set against a balustrade crowded with ordinary Madrid townspeople, *majas*, children and beggars, with angels below rather than above. It is considered one of the founding works of modern painting.

To protect the frescoes the chapel was retired from worship, and an identical chapel was built alongside in 1925 for services. Goya died in exile in Bordeaux in 1828; his remains were brought back to Spain in 1919 and reburied beneath the dome he had painted — arriving, according to the disinterment record, without their skull.

#### Iglesia de San Antonio de los Alemanes
*Church · Visit 10/24*

Founded by Philip III in 1606 as the chapel of a hospice for Portuguese subjects of the Spanish crown (Portugal and Spain shared a king from 1580 to 1640), and built from 1624 by Pedro Sánchez and Francisco Bautista on a rare oval plan. When Portugal regained independence in 1640 the institution lost its purpose; in 1689 the widowed queen Mariana of Austria reassigned it to Madrid's German Catholic community, giving it its present name.

The unassuming exterior hides one of the most completely frescoed interiors in Spain: the walls and the oval dome are covered, floor to lantern, with illusionistic architecture and scenes from the life of St Anthony, painted from the 1660s by Francisco Rizi, Juan Carreño de Miranda and the Italian quadratura specialist Michele Colonna. It still functions as a church and charitable soup kitchen run by the Hermandad del Refugio.

#### Museo Reina Sofía
*Museum · Visit 10/23*

Spain's national museum of 20th- and 21st-century art occupies the former Hospital General de Madrid, a huge Neoclassical building begun under Philip II, largely rebuilt from 1776 by José de Hermosilla and Francesco Sabatini, and used as a hospital until 1965. Converted into an arts centre in 1986 and given full national-museum status in 1992, it was extended in 2005 by a Jean Nouvel wing around a red courtyard.

Its central work is Picasso's *Guernica* (1937), painted for the Spanish Republic's pavilion at the 1937 Paris Exposition in response to the German and Italian bombing of the Basque town; it hung at MoMA in New York for decades at Picasso's insistence and came to Spain only in 1981, after the return of democracy, reaching the Reina Sofía in 1992. The collection is also strong in Miró, Dalí, Juan Gris, and the Spanish avant-gardes before and after the Civil War.

## Toledo

A day trip from Madrid; no overnight stay.

Toledo sits in a granite loop of the river Tagus, a natural fortress that has been the political and religious centre of the Iberian interior for two thousand years. It was the Roman *Toletum*, then the capital of the Visigothic kingdom from the 6th century until the Muslim conquest of 711 — the councils of Toledo effectively legislated for the whole peninsula. Under Muslim rule it was a frontier city; Alfonso VI of Castile took it in 1085, and it became the seat of the Primate of Spain and, for long stretches, the residence of the Castilian court. It is often called the "City of Three Cultures" for the coexistence — always unequal, and ended by the expulsions of Jews in 1492 and Muslims later — of Christians, Jews and Muslims, whose builders created the hybrid *Mudéjar* style. When Philip II fixed the capital at Madrid in 1561 Toledo stopped growing, which is why its medieval and Renaissance townscape survives so intact; the whole city is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

#### Toledo Cathedral (Catedral Primada)
*Sight · Visit 10/25*

The seat of the Archbishop of Toledo, Primate of Spain. Begun in 1226 under Ferdinand III and Archbishop Rodrigo Jiménez de Rada on the site of the main mosque (itself built over a Visigothic church), it is one of the three great 13th-century High Gothic cathedrals of Spain and, for many, the finest. Construction ran for more than two and a half centuries, to a consecration in 1493; the master masons ranged from the Frenchman Martín to Petrus Petri, who introduced the mature French Gothic of the triforium and vaults.

Because it was built so slowly and enriched by so many wealthy archbishops, it is really a museum of Spanish art in its own right: the 15th-century carved and painted high altarpiece; the two-tier choir stalls, the lower row carved with scenes of the conquest of Granada; the silver-gilt monstrance of Enrique de Arfe (early 16th century) carried through the streets at Corpus Christi; and the *Transparente* (1729–32), a Baroque explosion of marble, bronze and painted figures by Narciso Tomé pierced by a hole cut in the ambulatory vault to light the tabernacle. The sacristy functions as a picture gallery, with El Greco's *Disrobing of Christ* (*El Expolio*), Goya, Caravaggio, Titian, Van Dyck and Bellini.

#### Alcázar de Toledo
*Sight · Visit 10/25*

The stone quadrangle with corner towers crowning the highest point of the city stands where the Romans, Visigoths and Muslim governors all kept a fortress, and where the Castilian kings held court after 1085. Charles V had it rebuilt from the 1540s as a Renaissance palace by Alonso de Covarrubias and, later, Juan de Herrera; the north and south façades date from that campaign.

It was ruined and rebuilt repeatedly — sacked in the War of the Spanish Succession, burned by the British in the Peninsular War, burned again in 1887 — and served as a military academy. Its modern fame rests on the Siege of the Alcázar in 1936: at the start of the Civil War a Nationalist garrison and their families held the fortress against Republican forces from 21 July to 27 September, when Franco diverted his advancing army to relieve it. The episode, including the story of the commander Colonel Moscardó refusing to surrender when his captured son was threatened with death, was turned into a founding legend of the Franco regime, and the shattered building was rebuilt as a monument to it. Since 2010 it houses the Museo del Ejército (Army Museum).

#### Museo del Greco
*Museum · Visit 10/25*

Doménikos Theotokópoulos, "El Greco" (1541–1614), the Cretan-born painter trained in Venice and Rome who settled in Toledo about 1577 and never left, is not represented by his actual house — that had already vanished. In 1911 the collector and cultural patron Benigno de la Vega-Inclán, 2nd Marqués de la Vega-Inclán, bought and restored an adjacent 16th-century house complex, furnished it in period style, and opened it as a museum evoking the painter's domestic world — one of the first "recreated artist's house" museums in Spain. It holds a *View and Plan of Toledo*, an *Apostolado* series, and works by his son and workshop; it passed to the Spanish state on the marquis's death in 1942.

#### Iglesia de Santo Tomé
*Church · Visit 10/25*

A modest parish church, 12th century in origin, rebuilt around 1300 at the expense of Gonzalo Ruiz de Toledo, lord of Orgaz, and given the slender brick *Mudéjar* bell-tower — probably reusing a mosque minaret — that is a landmark of the skyline. In 1586 the parish commissioned El Greco to paint, for a side chapel, *The Burial of the Count of Orgaz*: the legend that when the pious count was buried in 1323, Saints Augustine and Stephen descended to lower his body into the tomb. El Greco divided the canvas into an earthly register — a frieze of contemporary Toledan gentlemen in black, portraits of the city's elite (and the painter's son, and reputedly himself) — and a visionary heaven above. It has never left the church, which today admits visitors to that chapel by ticket.

#### Mirador del Valle
*Viewpoint · Visit 10/25*

A belvedere on the Carretera de Circunvalación across the Tagus gorge, on the hills south of the city. It gives the classic panorama of Toledo rising in a single mass above the river, cathedral spire and Alcázar block dominating — essentially the view El Greco composed in his *View of Toledo* and *View and Plan*. Best at sunset and after dark, when the monuments are lit.

#### Puente de San Martín
*Bridge · Visit 10/25*

A fortified medieval bridge carrying the road into the old town from the west, over the Tagus gorge, complementing the Roman-founded Puente de Alcántara on the east. Its central arch spans about 40 metres — one of the widest bridge arches in the world when built — and it is guarded by crenellated gate-towers. It was raised in the late 14th century, largely at the initiative of Archbishop Pedro Tenorio, after floods destroyed an earlier crossing around 1380. A local legend tells of the master builder's wife who, learning that a miscalculation would bring the bridge down at its unveiling, set fire to the scaffolding herself to force a rebuild and save her husband's reputation.

## Camino Francés

The final stretch of the Camino Francés, walking from Sarria to Santiago de Compostela — about 115 km over 9 days.

The cult of Santiago (Saint James the Greater, apostle and, by legend, evangelist of Spain) began around 820, when — as the tradition runs — a hermit named Pelayo, guided by lights over a wood, found a Roman-era tomb near Iria Flavia in Galicia. Bishop Theodomir declared the bones those of the apostle, and King Alfonso II of Asturias walked from Oviedo to venerate them, founding a church on the spot; the place took the name *Compostela*. By the 11th and 12th centuries, with the Asturian-Leonese and then Castilian kings actively promoting it as a counterweight to Muslim al-Andalus, Santiago had become, with Rome and Jerusalem, one of the three supreme pilgrimage goals of Latin Christendom. A road system across northern Spain — the *Camino Francés*, "French Road," so called because it carried pilgrims coming over the Pyrenees from France — developed with its own bridges, hospices, monasteries and, in the 1130s, a written guide: Book V of the *Codex Calixtinus*, effectively the first European guidebook, which describes the stages, peoples, rivers and shrines along the way.

The Camino declined after the Reformation and the wars of the early modern period and was near-moribund by the 20th century; its revival dates from the 1980s (helped by the Council of Europe naming it the first European Cultural Route in 1987, and by Pope John Paul II's visits), and numbers have risen from a few hundred a year to hundreds of thousands. Sarria is the busiest starting point because it lies just over 100 km from the cathedral — the minimum walking distance to earn the *Compostela*, the Latin certificate of pilgrimage the cathedral has issued in some form since the Middle Ages.

### Sarria

Lodging: Pensión-Albergue Puente Ribeira (Rua do Peregrino, 23, 27600 Sarria, Spain)

Sarria as a town was effectively created by Alfonso IX, king of León and Galicia, who chartered *Vilanova de Sarria* here as part of a deliberate policy of founding and repopulating towns along the pilgrim road. He died in Sarria in 1230, on pilgrimage to Santiago himself — he is said to have made the journey more often than any other medieval monarch. The hilltop *rúa Maior* preserves the medieval layout, with the church of El Salvador (13th century, Romanesque portal), the ruined keep of the fortress torn down in the 15th-century *Irmandiño* revolts, and, at the edge of town, the Augustinian convent and hospital of A Madalena, founded in the 13th century by Italian priests to shelter pilgrims and still a monastery.

For most walkers Sarria is not a destination but a threshold: it is the last town more than 100 km from Santiago on the Camino Francés, so it is where the largest share of pilgrims begin, and where the character of the last week — busier, more social, more commercial — is set.

### Portomarín

Lodging: Pension Perez (Praza Aviacion Espanola 2, 27170 Portomarín, Spain)

The Portomarín you walk into is barely sixty years old. The historic town stood on both banks of the Miño at a strategic ford and bridge mentioned in the *Codex Calixtinus*, with two fortified Romanesque churches and medieval commanderies of the military orders of Santiago, the Temple and the Hospitallers of St John. In 1962 the Belesar dam flooded the valley, and the old town was drowned. Before the water rose, the most important monuments were dismantled stone by stone — each block numbered — and rebuilt on the hill of Monte do Cristo above the new reservoir: above all the fortress-church of San Nicolás (also called San Xoán), a late-12th-/early-13th-century building of the Hospitaller knights, battlemented like a castle, with a carved Romanesque portal attributed to the workshop of Master Mateo. In dry summers the stumps of the old bridge and quays reappear above the waterline. The steep staircase up into the town, past a surviving arch of the old bridge, is the pilgrim's entry.

### Gonzar

Lodging: Hosteria de Gonzar (3 Gonzar, 27188 Gonzar, Spain)

A hamlet on the high, open farmland of the *meseta lucense*, in the parish of Gonzar (municipality of Portomarín). It appears in medieval pilgrim itineraries as a staging point and has a small Romanesque church, Santa María de Gonzar. It has no urban history to speak of; its role, then as now, is to offer a bed and a meal to pilgrims who want to break the long stretch between Portomarín and Palas de Rei.

### Palas de Rei

Lodging: Pension Pardellas (Rua do Mercado, nº 13, 27200 Palas de Rei, Spain)

The name is usually derived from *Pallatium Regis*, "the king's palace," and linked by tradition to a residence of the Visigothic king Witiza (reigned c. 702–710) in this area. Whatever the truth of that, the town's growth was driven by the Camino: it is named in the *Codex Calixtinus* as the end of the eleventh stage, the last before the final push to Santiago. The surrounding *concello* is rich in Romanesque churches and fortified houses; the outstanding monument, a short detour off the route, is the church of Vilar de Donas, mother house and burial church of the Galician knights of the Order of Santiago, with 14th-century tomb effigies and Gothic wall paintings.

### Melide

Lodging: Pensión Pereiro (Calle Progreso 43, 15800 Melide, Spain)

Melide grew at the junction where the Camino Primitivo — the original 9th-century route from Oviedo taken by Alfonso II — joins the Camino Francés, which made it an important medieval waypoint with pilgrim hospitals and a now-vanished castle (demolished, like Sarria's, in the 15th-century revolts). On the western edge of town stands the Cruceiro de Melide, generally held to be the oldest surviving stone wayside cross in Galicia (14th century), with a Crucifixion on one face and a seated Christ in Majesty on the other. Melide is also, improbably for an inland town, the recognised capital of *pulpo á feira* — octopus boiled in copper cauldrons, cut with scissors, dressed with paprika, coarse salt and olive oil, and eaten on wooden plates in dedicated *pulperías* — a trade that grew from its role as a regional market and a stop on cattle- and fish-droving routes.

### Arzúa

Lodging: Casa do Cabo (Rúa Alcalde Juan Vidal, n13-15, 15810 Arzúa, Spain)

The last town of any size before Santiago, about 39 km out, and the point where the Camino del Norte (the coastal route) merges into the Francés. Its consolidation as a town, again, tracks the 11th–12th-century boom of the pilgrimage. Monuments are modest — the Gothic chapel of A Madalena, remains of an Augustinian convent, the rebuilt parish church of Santiago — but Arzúa is the centre of a protected-origin cheese, *Queixo Arzúa-Ulloa*, a soft, mild cow's-milk cheese sold everywhere in town.

### O Pedrouzo

Lodging: Pensión A Solaina 3 (Rúa Minas Numero 2, Portal 1, 2 A, 15821 O Pedrouzo, Spain)

O Pedrouzo (also called Arca, and the seat of the municipality of O Pino) is a roadside village about 20 km from Santiago that exists on the pilgrim map almost entirely as the practical last overnight stop before the finish — near enough to reach the cathedral in a morning, before the noon Pilgrim's Mass. The parish church of Santa Eulalia de Arca is a Neoclassical rebuilding of the 19th century after fire destroyed the earlier chapel.

### The Cathedral of Santiago de Compostela

Lodging (11/02): Parador de Santiago de Compostela — Hostal dos Reis Católicos (Praza do Obradoiro, 1, 15705 Santiago de Compostela, Spain)
Lodging (11/03): PR Paz de Agra (Calderería, 37, 15704 Santiago de Compostela, Spain)

Construction of the present cathedral began in 1075 under Alfonso VI and Bishop Diego Peláez, replacing the earlier churches over the tomb. It is one of the great Romanesque pilgrimage churches of Europe — a barrel-vaulted, aisled, cruciform basilica with an ambulatory designed to move crowds of pilgrims past the shrine — and it was substantially complete by 1211. Its supreme sculptural work is the **Pórtico da Gloria**, the inner west portal carved by Master Mateo and his workshop and finished in 1188: some two hundred figures of the Last Judgement and the heavenly Jerusalem, with a smiling column of prophets and a seated St James welcoming the arriving pilgrim, marking the transition from Romanesque to Gothic.

Later centuries wrapped the Romanesque core in other styles: Gothic and Plateresque cloisters and chapels, and above all the **Obradoiro façade** (1738–50) by Fernando de Casas Novoa, a soaring granite Baroque screen — one of the masterpieces of Spanish Baroque — built to protect the Pórtico and give the cathedral a theatrical face onto its square. Inside, the *botafumeiro*, a 53-kg silver-plated censer swung the width of the transept by eight men, is used on major feast days and, historically, to fumigate a church full of travel-worn pilgrims. The relics of the apostle — whose historical identity with the tomb is a matter of faith, not evidence — lie in a silver casket in the crypt beneath the high altar.

The **Hostal dos Reis Católicos** on the north side of the Praza do Obradoiro was founded in 1499 by Ferdinand and Isabella, after their own pilgrimage, as the Gran Hospital Real: a hospital and hostel for pilgrims arriving footsore or sick at the end of the road. Enrique Egas built it from 1501 around four cloistered courtyards, with a richly carved Plateresque portal. It cared for pilgrims into the 20th century and became a state-run *parador* (historic hotel) in 1954; it still, by tradition, offers a free meal to the first pilgrims presenting their *Compostela* each day. The old town around it — largely rebuilt in granite in the 17th and 18th centuries over its medieval street plan — is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Barcelona

Lodging: Rooms Borrells, Carrer del Comte Borrell, 9, 08015 Barcelona, Spain

The last and longest-running stop of the trip in historical terms. Barcelona was founded around 10 BC as the Roman colony *Colonia Iulia Augusta Faventia Paterna Barcino*, a small walled town on a low rise (the Mons Táber) near the sea. After Visigothic and a brief Muslim period, it became the seat of the Counts of Barcelona, who from the 12th century were also Kings of Aragon; the "Crown of Aragon" made the city the capital of a Mediterranean trading empire reaching to Sicily, Sardinia, Naples and Athens, and gave it its great age of civil Gothic building and its representative institutions (the *Consell de Cent*, the *Generalitat*). Union with Castile (1479) and the shift of empire to the Atlantic pushed Barcelona to the margins; it backed the losing side in the War of the Spanish Succession, and its surrender on 11 September 1714 to the Bourbon Philip V was followed by the abolition of Catalan self-government, the razing of a whole district to build a fortress, and a ban on the university.

The city's modern trajectory begins with 19th-century industrialisation — Catalonia was the first industrial region in Spain — which brought population, a militant labour movement, a wealthy bourgeoisie, and the demolition of the walls (from 1854) that allowed the gridded *Eixample* to spread inland. Cultural and political self-assertion revived in parallel: the *Renaixença* revalued the Catalan language and past, and *Modernisme* — Gaudí, Domènech i Montaner, Puig i Cadafalch — gave the new city an architecture of its own. The 20th century brought anarcho-syndicalism, the short-lived autonomy of the Second Republic, the Civil War (Barcelona was a Republican stronghold, heavily bombed and then occupied in 1939), and forty years of Franco-era repression of Catalan identity. Democracy after 1975 restored the Generalitat, and the 1992 Olympic Games funded a wholesale reinvention of the city that reopened its long-walled-off seafront.

### Gothic Quarter & El Born

The stone heart of the old city on the Roman Mons Táber, and the medieval merchant quarter of the Ribera beside it — Roman walls and the counts' palace, a Gothic cathedral, the guild streets of El Born — wrapped by the Rambla and the once-notorious Raval.

#### La Rambla
*Street · Visit 11/04*

The Rambla follows the bed of a seasonal watercourse (Arabic *ramla*, "sandy riverbed") that ran outside the 13th-century city wall. Over the 16th and 17th centuries convents, colleges and a theatre were built along its landward side; when the wall was demolished after 1704 and the promenade formally laid out from 1766, those religious houses gave their names to its successive stretches — Rambla dels Estudis (the old university), de Sant Josep or "de les Flors" (the flower stalls), dels Caputxins, de Santa Mònica. Through the 19th century it became the city's social spine, lined with the Liceu opera house (1847), the Boqueria market, newspaper kiosks, cafés and the Miró pavement mosaic at Pla de l'Os. Long the promenade where, in George Orwell's phrase, all Barcelona came out to walk, it is today heavily given over to tourism.

#### Plaça Reial
*Plaza · Visit 11/04*

A regular, arcaded square just off the Rambla, built on the site of a Capuchin friary dissolved in the 1830s. Francesc Daniel Molina designed it (built 1848–59) on the model of the uniform residential squares of Bourbon France, with a central fountain of the Three Graces and palm trees. The two ornate cast-iron lampposts flanking the fountain, with winged helmets and coiled serpents, are the first public commission of the young Antoni Gaudí (1879). Once a genteel address, then seedy, it is now a nightlife hub.

#### El Raval
*District · Visit 11/04*

The wedge of the old town west of the Rambla — the name means "the outskirts" — lay outside the first medieval wall and was enclosed only by Peter IV's 14th-century wall, after which it filled with convents, hospitals, orphanages and other institutions the walled city preferred to keep at arm's length, along with market gardens. Industrialisation in the 19th century packed it with textile mills and tenements, making it the densest, poorest quarter in the city and a stronghold of the anarchist labour movement; the port-side end became notorious in the early 20th century as the *Barri Xino*, Barcelona's red-light district. Since the 1990s the city has driven regeneration by insertion — cutting the Rambla del Raval through the fabric, and planting the contemporary-art museum MACBA (Richard Meier, 1995) and the CCCB cultural centre — with mixed social results, in what remains the most diverse and immigrant neighbourhood of Barcelona.

#### Barri Gòtic
*District · Visit 11/05*

The core of the old town, on the Roman *Mons Táber*: fragments of Roman wall and towers, the temple of Augustus columns, medieval churches, guild halls, the Jewish quarter (the *Call*), and the royal and municipal palaces, all in grey stone. It is worth knowing that the "Gothic Quarter" as a tourist idea was largely constructed between about 1900 and the 1929 International Exposition: streets were widened, buildings "re-Gothicised" or moved, and some celebrated features — the neo-Gothic *Pont del Bisbe* bridge (1928), the cathedral façade — added outright. The medieval substance is real; the picturesque unity is partly a 20th-century composition.

#### MUHBA Plaça del Rei / Barcino
*Museum / site · Visit 11/05*

The main site of the Barcelona History Museum (MUHBA) is the Casa Padellàs, a Gothic merchant's house moved here stone by stone in the 1930s. Beneath it, excavations from that period opened up about 4,000 m² of the Roman and Visigothic city — one of the largest underground archaeological sites of any European city. Visitors walk Roman streets past the vats and floors of a *garum* (fish-sauce) factory, a laundry and dye works, a winery, and a 4th-century early-Christian complex, up to the Visigothic episcopal buildings, ending inside the medieval royal palace above.

#### Plaça del Rei
*Plaza · Visit 11/05*

A closed medieval square that was the courtyard of the palace of the Counts of Barcelona and Kings of Aragon. It is framed by the Palau Reial Major, the great hall (Saló del Tinell), the palatine chapel of Santa Àgata (1302, on the Roman wall), the 16th-century Palau del Lloctinent (the viceroy's residence, now the Crown of Aragon archive), and the tiered watchtower called the Mirador del Rei Martí (1555).

#### Palau Reial Major
*Palace · Visit 11/05*

The royal palace complex on Plaça del Rei, grown by accretion from the 11th to the 14th century: Romanesque lower walls, Gothic halls above, all raised against and on top of the Roman city wall, which forms its back. It was the working seat of comital and then royal power in Catalonia. When the court left for good, parts of it were handed to the Inquisition and to the royal courts of justice.

#### Saló del Tinell
*Palace · Visit 11/05*

The palace's Gothic throne and assembly hall, built 1359–70 by Guillem Carbonell for Peter IV ("the Ceremonious"). Its 17-metre-wide space is spanned without columns by six enormous semicircular stone *diaphragm* arches carrying a timber roof — a hallmark of Catalan civil Gothic, prizing broad, plain, load-bearing volume. It housed the Catalan *Corts* (parliament) and royal ceremonies; tradition — probably invented later — holds that Ferdinand and Isabella received Columbus here on his return from the Americas in 1493. Later walled up inside a Baroque church, it was rediscovered and restored in the 1930s and is now an exhibition hall.

#### Barcelona Cathedral (La Seu)
*Church · Visit 11/05*

Dedicated to the Holy Cross and to Saint Eulalia — a 13-year-old martyred, by tradition, under Diocletian, and the city's co-patron, whose relics lie in the crypt. The Gothic cathedral was begun on 1 May 1298 under James II, built east to west, and largely finished by 1417, with the cloister — where thirteen white geese are kept, one for each year of Eulalia's life — completed in 1448. The elaborate neo-Gothic west front and central spire, often taken for medieval, were added in 1887–1913 to a 15th-century paper design, paid for by the industrialist Manuel Girona. The cloister's shaded garden, fountains and palm trees make it one of the quietest corners of the old town.

#### Carrer Montcada
*Street · Visit 11/05*

A straight street cut through the Ribera district in the 12th century, granted to the Montcada family for their service to Count Ramon Berenguer IV. From the 13th to the 16th century it was the address of Barcelona's merchant and noble elite, who lined it with Gothic and Renaissance palaces built around arcaded courtyards with external staircases. Several now hold museums: five adjoining palaces (Aguilar, Baró de Castellet, Meca and others) form the **Museu Picasso**; the Palau Dalmases and Palau dels Marquesos de Llió hold other collections.

#### La Llotja de Mar
*Building · Visit 11/05*

The merchants' exchange of medieval Barcelona. Peter IV authorised a covered trading hall around 1380; Pere Arvei built the Gothic **Saló de Contractacions** — three naves on slender columns under a timber roof, one of the finest civil Gothic interiors of the Mediterranean — in 1384–97, with the upper floor added in the 1450s for the *Consolat de Mar*, the maritime court whose body of case law influenced commercial custom across the medieval Mediterranean. The exterior was rebuilt in a sober Neoclassical style in the 1770s–90s. The Gothic hall survives intact within it; the building served as Barcelona's stock exchange until 1994 and belongs to the Chamber of Commerce.

#### El Born
*District · Visit 11/05*

The medieval artisans' and merchants' quarter around the basilica of Santa Maria del Mar. Its spine, the Passeig del Born, was the civic stage of the city from the 13th to the 18th century — jousts (*borns*), tournaments, markets, processions and public executions. Emptied and impoverished after 1714, it is now a district of galleries, designers and wine bars, with Santa Maria del Mar (1329–83), a church of a single, unaltered Catalan Gothic campaign, paid for by the parish's sailors and stevedores, at its heart.

#### El Born Centre de Cultura i Memòria
*Museum / site · Visit 11/05*

Housed in the Mercat del Born, an 1876 iron-and-glass market hall by Josep Fontserè, one of the first exposed-iron structures in the city. When the building was being converted to a library in 2001–02, workers uncovered, intact beneath the market floor, the streets, houses, wells and cisterns of the Ribera district as it stood in 1714 — the neighbourhood Philip V's army levelled to clear a field of fire for the Ciutadella fortress. Rather than build the library, the city preserved the excavation and opened it in 2013 as a memorial site interpreting Barcelona around 1700 and the siege, on the tercentenary's eve.

#### Fossar de les Moreres
*Memorial · Visit 11/05*

"The Mulberry Graveyard," a small square beside Santa Maria del Mar that was the parish cemetery and became the mass grave of the defenders killed in the final assault of 11 September 1714. A tradition holds that the gravedigger, Josep Coll, refused to bury pro-Bourbon dead among the fallen patriots. Cleared and paved in red brick in 1989 (Carme Fiol), and fitted in 2001 with a curling steel arm carrying an eternal flame, it is the focal point of the annual **Diada Nacional de Catalunya** on 11 September — a national day that commemorates a defeat.

### The Waterfront

The edge the city turned its back on for a century and reclaimed for the 1992 Olympics — the old harbour, the 18th-century fishermen's grid of Barceloneta, the fortress-turned-park of the Ciutadella, and the built-from-nothing Olympic seafront.

#### Port Vell
*Port · Visit 11/04*

The "old harbour" at the foot of the Rambla was, until the late 1980s, a working dockland of warehouses, rail yards and a coastal road that cut the city off from its own water. As part of the 1992 Olympic transformation it was rebuilt for leisure: the Moll de la Fusta promenade, the Maremagnum mall (1995) and aquarium on the Moll d'Espanya, and the undulating **Rambla de Mar** footbridge (1994) linking them to the Rambla. It is the clearest single example of the "return to the sea" that defined the Olympic-era city.

#### Barceloneta
*District · Visit 11/04*

A triangular spit of reclaimed land between the harbour and the open sea, laid out in 1753 as planned housing for families displaced when the Ribera district was demolished to build the Ciutadella fortress after 1714. Military engineers of the Bourbon crown (working from designs associated with Prosper de Verboom, executed under Juan Martín Cermeño) imposed a tight grid of long, narrow blocks, originally of low houses only two rooms deep so that every room had daylight and cross-ventilation. Traditionally a quarter of fishermen, dockers and sailors, it kept its intact 18th-century plan, its seafood restaurants and its dense working-class life; the city beach along its edge was created with imported sand for 1992.

#### Parc de la Ciutadella
*Park · Visit 11/05*

After 1714 Philip V razed about a fifth of the walled city — some 1,200 houses of the Ribera — to build a huge star-shaped fortress, the Ciutadella, aimed not outward at enemies but inward at Barcelona; it doubled as a political prison and was, for over a century, the most hated symbol of Bourbon rule. The city won its demolition in 1868 and turned the ground into its first public park, laid out by Josep Fontserè for the 1888 Universal Exposition — Barcelona's debut on the world stage. Fontserè's monumental cascade was built with the help of a student assistant, the young Gaudí. Three fort buildings survive; one now houses the Catalan parliament. The park also holds the zoo and several Modernista and Noucentista pavilions.

#### Vila Olímpica
*District · Visit 11/07*

The seafront neighbourhood built as the Olympic Village on the old Icària industrial zone of Poblenou, to a master plan by MBM (Martorell, Bohigas, Mackay). Building it meant demolishing derelict factories, burying the coastal railway, cleaning the shore and creating new beaches — opening a stretch of coast the city had been walled off from for a century. After the Games the athletes' flats were sold as housing. Frank Gehry's copper-mesh **Peix** ("Fish") sculpture marks the waterfront.

#### Port Olímpic
*Port · Visit 11/07*

The marina built beside the Vila Olímpica for the 1992 Olympic sailing events. Beside it stand the twin towers — the **Hotel Arts** (SOM / Bruce Graham) and the **Torre Mapfre** (Íñigo Ortiz and Enrique León) — both about 154 metres and, on completion in 1992–94, the tallest buildings in Barcelona and in Spain. Long a nightlife strip, the port's lower level was demolished and rebuilt from 2021 to shift it away from clubbing toward sailing, restaurants and public space.

#### Poblenou
*Urban district · Visit 11/07*

A seaward district that was the heart of Catalan industrialisation — so thick with textile mills, foundries and chemical works, served by two rail lines to the port, that it was called "the Catalan Manchester." Deindustrialisation from the 1960s left it derelict. The 1992 Olympics took the first bite (the Vila Olímpica); then the **22@** plan, approved by the city in 2000, rezoned some 200 hectares of old factory land for technology, media and research uses, universities and housing, keeping selected industrial buildings as heritage. The result is uneven — new offices next to surviving *fàbriques*, artists' studios and gentrification pressure — and much debated as a model of post-industrial renewal.

### Eixample & Modernisme

Cerdà's gridded 19th-century extension, and the showcase it became for Catalan Modernisme — Gaudí, Domènech i Montaner and Puig i Cadafalch building for the industrial bourgeoisie along and around the Passeig de Gràcia.

#### Eixample
*Urban district · Visit 11/06*

The gridded "Extension" that quintupled the size of Barcelona after the medieval walls were demolished from 1854. The engineer **Ildefons Cerdà** — who coined the word *urbanisme* — drew a rigorously egalitarian plan of 113-metre blocks with 45-degree chamfered corners (*xamfrans*) for light, air and (in his vision) trams and turning traffic, each block built up on only two sides around a planted communal courtyard, with hospitals, markets and green space evenly distributed. Madrid imposed Cerdà's plan over a locally preferred design in 1859–60. As built, speculation filled in the courtyards and raised the heights, so the social utopia was lost — but the grid, the chamfers and the long diagonal avenues remain the DNA of modern Barcelona, and became the canvas for Modernisme.

#### Palau de la Música Catalana
*Concert hall · Visit 11/06*

A concert hall built in 1905–08 by Lluís Domènech i Montaner for the *Orfeó Català*, a choral society founded in 1891 that was a leading vehicle of the Catalan cultural revival. Paid for by public subscription and bourgeois patronage, it is the fullest statement of Modernisme as a total art: a structure of exposed brick and iron dissolved into stained glass — including an inverted stained-glass dome that floods the auditorium with daylight — mosaic, sculpture (the half-relief muses of the stage wall) and ceramic flowers. It is the only auditorium listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site (1997) and is still the home of the Orfeó and a working concert hall.

#### Passeig de Gràcia
*Avenue · Visit 11/06*

The avenue began as the country road from walled Barcelona to the then-separate town of Gràcia, widened into a landscaped promenade in the 1820s. When the Eixample was built around it after 1860 it became the grandest address in the new city, where industrial and colonial fortunes built their showpiece houses — which is why the concentration of Modernista architecture here is unmatched. The wrought-iron-and-mosaic combined benches-and-streetlamps are by Pere Falqués (1906). Today it is also the luxury shopping street.

#### Casa Amatller
*Building · Visit 11/06*

Josep Puig i Cadafalch remodelled an 1870s building in 1898–1900 for the chocolate manufacturer Antoni Amatller — industrialist, art collector and pioneer photographer. Puig gave it a flat façade of coloured ceramic and sgraffito rising to a stepped gable of the Flemish-Dutch type, a learned historicist gesture; Eusebi Arnau carved the whimsical stone reliefs (including animals making chocolate and taking photographs). Standing right next to Gaudí's Casa Batlló, its deliberately different silhouette is half the reason the block is nicknamed the **Illa de la Discòrdia** ("Block of Discord"), where the three houses by Barcelona's rival Modernista architects — Puig, Gaudí, Domènech i Montaner — stand side by side.

#### Casa Batlló
*Building · Visit 11/06*

Gaudí's 1904–06 reconstruction of an ordinary 1877 apartment block for the textile industrialist Josep Batlló, who first wanted to demolish it. Gaudí kept the frame and transformed everything else: an undulating façade of broken tile and coloured glass (*trencadís*), balconies like skulls or carnival masks, columns like bones, and a ridged, scaled roof read as the back of a dragon pierced by Saint George's cross — hence the local name *Casa dels ossos*, "House of Bones." Inside, a light-well graded from white to deep blue tiles equalises daylight down the shaft. World Heritage since 2005.

#### La Pedrera / Casa Milà
*Building · Visit 11/06*

Gaudí's last secular building, an apartment house of 1906–12 for Pere Milà and Roser Segimon. Its self-supporting stone façade curls in wavelike bays with almost no straight line, wrapped in seaweed-like wrought-iron balconies by Josep Maria Jujol; the plan hangs from stone and brick arches and iron beams, freeing the interior of load-bearing walls; the roof terrace is a landscape of sculpted chimneys and vents, some helmeted like warriors. The satirical press mocked it as a quarry (*pedrera*) and a hangar for airships, and Gaudí sued the owners over his fee. World Heritage; the top floors and roof are a museum, the rest still lived in.

#### Sagrada Família
*Church · Visit 11/06*

An *expiatory temple* — funded only by donations and alms — begun in 1882 to a conventional neo-Gothic design by Francisco de Paula del Villar. When Villar resigned in 1883 the commission passed to the 31-year-old Gaudí, who redesigned it completely and, especially after about 1914, gave the rest of his life to it, living on the site. He worked out its structure — leaning columns branching like trees, hyperboloid vaults, catenary arches modelled with hanging weighted strings — as a system in which the building stands with almost no buttressing, and conceived its three great façades (Nativity, Passion, Glory) and eighteen towers as a catechism in stone. At his death under a tram in 1926 only the crypt, apse and one Nativity tower were up. Work continued from his models and drawings (many lost in a 1936 anarchist fire and since reconstructed), long controversial among architects; computer modelling and donation and ticket income accelerated it, and in 2010 Benedict XVI consecrated it as a basilica. The central Tower of Jesus Christ, topping out around 2026 at some 172 metres, makes it the tallest church in the world.

#### Avinguda de Gaudí
*Avenue · Visit 11/06*

A short diagonal avenue slashed across the Eixample grid in the early 20th century to connect Gaudí's Sagrada Família with Domènech i Montaner's Hospital de Sant Pau — deliberately aligning the city's two Modernista landmarks on a single axis. It is pedestrian-friendly, lined with terraces and Falqués-style lamps.

#### Sant Pau Recinte Modernista
*Building complex · Visit 11/06*

The Hospital de la Santa Creu i Sant Pau, funded by a bequest from the banker Pau Gil and built by Lluís Domènech i Montaner from 1902, completed by his son Pere Domènech i Roure in 1930. Rejecting the hospital-as-single-block, Domènech laid out 27 free-standing pavilions in a garden, oriented for sun and air and linked by a kilometre of service tunnels, each richly finished in brick, ceramic, mosaic and sculpture on the theory that beauty aids recovery. It served as a working hospital until 2009, when clinical functions moved to a new building alongside; the historic precinct was restored and opened as a museum and offices. World Heritage (1997), jointly listed with the Palau de la Música.

### Montjuïc & Poble-sec

The harbour hill and the working-class district at its foot — a fortress that shelled its own city, the citizen-dug shelters and hilltop batteries of the Civil War, and the 1929 Exposition and 1992 Olympic grounds laid over them.

#### Poble-sec
*District · Visit 11/07*

"The dry village," the first planned working-class suburb outside the walls, laid out on the flank of Montjuïc from the 1850s–60s; the name is usually explained by its long wait for a public fountain (arriving 1894). Wedged between the factories of the port and the hill, it was a poor, dense, politically radical quarter, and in 1937–38 one of the most bombed parts of Barcelona, honeycombed with citizen-built air-raid shelters. Today it is known for the tapas street Carrer de Blai and for theatres (El Molino, the Grec).

#### Refugi 307
*Air-raid shelter · Visit 11/07*

Barcelona was one of the first cities in the world subjected to sustained aerial bombing of civilians — chiefly by the Italian air force flying from Mallorca, most devastatingly on 16–18 March 1938. The Republican city organised a *Junta de Defensa Passiva* and, crucially, residents dug their own shelters: over 1,300 across the city. Refugi 307, tunnelled into Montjuïc by the people of Poble-sec from 1937, runs about 400 metres, with vaulted soft-brick passages (designed to absorb shock waves), a water source, latrines, an infirmary and a nursery, for around 2,000 people. It is preserved and shown by the city history museum as the best-surviving example.

#### Montjuïc
*Hill · Visit 11/07*

The flat-topped hill over the harbour, probably named "Mountain of the Jews" for the medieval Jewish cemetery on its slopes (9th–14th centuries; hundreds of graves have been excavated). Its summit has been fortified since the Reapers' War of 1640; the present star fort largely dates from the 1750s and, aimed down at the city, became a hated instrument of central control — it bombarded Barcelona in 1842, and under Franco its moat and yards were an execution ground, where the president of the Catalan government, **Lluís Companys**, extradited by the Gestapo, was shot on 15 October 1940. The hill also carries the terraced halls, fountains and the "Spanish Village" of the 1929 International Exposition, and the 1992 Olympic venues; the castle was handed to the city in 2007 and is being reinterpreted as a peace and memory centre.

#### Olympic Ring (Anella Olímpica)
*Olympic venue area · Visit 11/07*

The cluster of venues built or rebuilt on Montjuïc for the 1992 Games: the Olympic Stadium, the **Palau Sant Jordi** indoor arena (Arata Isozaki, 1990, its dome raised into place by hydraulic jacks), the Picornell swimming pools, the INEFC sports university (Ricardo Bofill), and Santiago Calatrava's white **Torre de Comunicacions** (1992), a 136-metre leaning mast shaped like a kneeling figure holding the Olympic flame, doubling as a giant sundial. The ensemble, on a long terrace with city views, is the built emblem of the "Barcelona model" of using a mega-event to remake a city.

#### Estadi Olímpic Lluís Companys
*Stadium · Visit 11/07*

Built by Pere Domènech i Roura for the 1929 Exposition, and intended to host the 1936 "People's Olympiad" — an anti-fascist counter-Games to the Berlin Olympics — which was cancelled when the Civil War broke out the day before the opening. For 1992 the stadium was gutted and rebuilt behind its retained 1929 façade, its pitch dug down to add seating (Gregotti with Correa, Milà, Margarit, Buixadé). It was renamed in 1992 for Lluís Companys, the Catalan president executed on the hill above in 1940.

#### Turó de la Rovira
*Anti-aircraft battery · Visit 11/07*

A 260-metre hilltop east of the centre with a 360-degree field of fire, chosen by the Republican air-defence command for an anti-aircraft battery in 1937 — four Vickers guns, operational by early 1938 — which with the batteries on Montjuïc and in Poblenou formed a "triangle of fire" over the city. After the war the abandoned concrete emplacements were roofed and inhabited: a shantytown, *Els Canons*, of up to a thousand people lived here into the 1990s. The gun platforms and traces of the shacks survive; the city history museum interprets the site, which has also become one of the most popular free viewpoints over Barcelona ("the bunkers").
